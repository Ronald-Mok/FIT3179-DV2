Business innovation in Australia

The State of Innovation: Inside Australian Businesses

How Australian businesses innovate, who leads, and what holds the rest back.

By Mok Weng Cheong Ronald · Updated 24 October 2026 · Data: ABS, IP Australia, WIPO, OECD

Almost half of Australian businesses tried something new in 2024–25, from a café taking online orders to a manufacturer launching a new product. Seven questions take you through who is innovating, where, how it has changed, and what is stopping the rest.

01

What is business innovation?

Any new or improved product, or new way of running a business, counts. Innovation comes in two types.

Goods & services innovation

Selling something new or noticeably improved.

  • A bakery launches a gluten-free range.
  • A bank releases a new mobile app.

Process innovation

A new or improved way of working: making, delivering, marketing, IT systems or management.

  • A café starts taking online orders.
  • A builder moves to new scheduling software.

Innovation-active

A business that introduced an innovation, or was still working on one (including attempts it abandoned). This is the main measure used on this page.

The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) follows the Oslo Manual, the international guide to measuring innovation written by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), a group of mostly high-income countries that includes Australia, and Eurostat, the European Union's statistics office.

The chart counts innovations, not businesses. A business that introduced a new service and a new marketing method counts once for each. Hover over a slice to see how many businesses introduced it.

So how many Australian businesses are actually doing this?

02

How innovative are Australian businesses?

Almost half are innovation-active, and Australia sits in the world's top 20 on innovation.

At home

46%

of Australian businesses were innovation-active in 2024–25.

Pick a business size under the chart: the bigger the business, the more likely it is to innovate.

Against the world

Innovation tends to gather in cities. WIPO, the United Nations agency for patents and other intellectual property, ranks the world's top 100 innovation clusters by their patents, scientific articles and venture capital deals.

Australia has two: Sydney (38th) and Melbourne (53rd). Both hold a bigger share of the world's scientific articles and venture capital deals than of its patents.

Nearly half of businesses are innovating, but not evenly. Which industries lead?

03

Which industries are leading innovation?

The industries that innovate most often aren't the ones that spend the most on research.

Retail has the highest share of innovation-active businesses (59%), but professional, scientific and technical services account for 38% of all business spending on research and development (R&D).

Industry is only part of the story. Does the size of a business matter?

04

Does business size matter?

Yes. 71% of large businesses are innovation-active, but only 39% of micro businesses.

Size matters. Does location?

05

How do the states compare?

Business R&D is concentrated in New South Wales and Victoria.

To compare states fairly, R&D spending is shown as a share of each state's economy (its gross state product), not as a dollar total.

Each measure tells a different part of the story, so the scorecard ranks every state on all of them at once.

No state leads on everything. The Northern Territory comes first for innovation and AI use but last for R&D and patents, while the ACT files the most patents per person.

These patterns didn't appear overnight. How has innovation changed over time?

06

How has innovation changed over time?

More businesses innovate than in 2006–07, though the way it is measured has changed along the way.

The ABS changed its survey twice: new definitions from 2018–19, and from 2024–25 a question about the past year instead of the past two.

A change in the question can move the number on its own, so the dashed links mark steps that are not like-for-like. Read the long-run rise, not the size of any single step.

Business spending on R&D has stayed at about 0.9% of the economy since 2017–18. Across the OECD, the group of mostly high-income countries that Australia belongs to, the average has risen.

More businesses want to innovate. So what's stopping the rest?

07

What prevents businesses from innovating?

Money and people, not ideas. A lack of funds and a lack of skilled staff are the most common barriers.

About one in three businesses (35%) reported a barrier to innovation in 2024–25. Few say they lack the know-how: only 3.9% named a lack of knowledge or technology.

Size changes the answer. Micro businesses are most often short of money; from five staff up, finding skilled people is the bigger problem.

Fewer businesses report barriers than in 2022–23, led by a big fall in skill shortages.

Part of the drop comes from the survey itself: 2022–23 asked about two years, 2024–25 about one, and a shorter period gives fewer chances to hit a barrier.

Key findings

1

Innovation is common

Almost half of businesses do it, mostly by changing how they work.

2

Different industries lead

Retailers innovate most often; scientific and technical services spend the most.

3

Size matters

Large businesses are almost twice as likely to innovate as micro businesses.

4

Place matters

R&D spending is concentrated in New South Wales and Victoria.

5

Money and skills hold it back

Innovation has grown but R&D spending hasn't, and funds and skilled staff are the main barriers.

What it means for you

If you work in a business

Skills are in short supply: a lack of skilled staff is the top barrier for every business size from five staff up.

If you run a small business

You are not alone: funding is the most common barrier for micro businesses, and most innovation is about changing how you work, not inventing something new.

As a customer

The shops, cafés and services you use most are among the most innovative industries, so expect new ways to buy and be served.

About

About this visualisation

Domain
Business innovation in Australia: how many businesses introduce new or improved products or ways of working, which industries, business sizes and states lead, how this has changed over time, and what holds businesses back.
Who it's for
Everyday Australians, including small-business owners and their staff. No statistics background is needed: terms such as "innovation-active" and "R&D" are explained where they first appear.
What (data)
The latest official figures: the ABS Characteristics of Australian Business, 2024–25 and business R&D survey, earlier ABS innovation surveys (through the Department of Industry's Australian Innovation Statistics), the WIPO Global Innovation Index 2026, IP Australia patent and trade mark records, OECD R&D statistics, and Natural Earth and ABS boundaries for the maps. Most values are shares of businesses, dollars, or index scores and ranks.
Why (purpose)
To let readers compare innovation across industries, business sizes, states and countries; see how the parts make up the whole; find where innovation happens; follow how it has changed; and identify what stops businesses from innovating.
How (charts)
18 Vega-Lite charts, including five kinds of map (choropleth, proportional symbol, flow, dot and bin maps) and a sunburst, waffle, heatmaps, lollipop, Marimekko, dumbbells, line, bump, slope and bar chart. Colour keeps one meaning throughout: darker blue means more, amber and green are the two types of innovation, orange marks Australia, and a blue-grey ramp runs from micro to large businesses. The bump chart uses blue, green and dark red only to pick out the industries the text talks about. All colours come from colour-blind-safe palettes (ColorBrewer Blues and Okabe–Ito). Maps use equal-area projections, and tooltips, drop-downs and buttons add detail where it helps.
